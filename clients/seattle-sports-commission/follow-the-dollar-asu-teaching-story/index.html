Why does $9.9M turn into $16.6M?

The $9.9M is what card networks actually recorded. The $16.6M is a modeled estimate that adds what the cards can't see, like cash, spending re-circulating through local payrolls and suppliers, and activity outside the tracked area. Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions, and a good data engineer never lets them sit in the same column without a label.

The insight

Stadium games produced about $55 and $47 per attendee. The parade produced about $4.80, even though it drew roughly nine times the crowd. Big crowds and big spending aren't the same thing. A parade is free, short and outdoors, so people come, cheer and go home. A playoff game is ticketed, hours long and surrounded by bars and restaurants.

Ask yourself: if you were deciding which event to fund with tourism dollars, which metric would you lead with, total attendance or spend per attendee? What would each choice reward?

05

Built for the Seattle Sports Commission

One framework. One annual number. One stronger case for Seattle sports.

A dashboard tells you what happened at one event. The Standardized Sports Economic Impact Model is designed to answer two harder questions every year, the same way, so the answers can be compared and defended.

Q1

How much economic value does Seattle sports generate annually?

Q2

How much STIA funding should the Seattle Sports Commission receive based on that contribution?

The goal is a baseline, not a perfect study

This model is designed to produce the minimum defensible annual impact. It isn't a one-time report or a FIFA-scale analysis. It's built to be:

  • Repeatable
  • Standardized
  • Defensible
  • Expandable
  • Updated annually
  • Data-driven

That word "minimum" matters. When you walk into a budget conversation with a conservative number, nobody can knock it down. You build credibility first, then expand.

The funding objective

Seattle Tourism Improvement Area

~$23M–$24M

Current annual fund, generated through a 2.3% hotel assessment and administered by Visit Seattle.

Remember: lodging is 83% visitor spend. Hotel guests fund STIA. If sports brings those guests, sports has a case for a share.

Case study · Seattle Metro Chamber & Kent Chamber

The World Cup came to Lumen Field. We followed it into the neighborhoods.

From June 14 to July 19, 2026, ten funded watch parties and business activations ran across seven South King County communities: Kent, SeaTac, Burien, Federal Way, White Center, Skyway and Maple Valley–Black Diamond. The question wasn't "how big was the World Cup?" It was "did the money reach small businesses, and what should the next round fund?"

Your path

You don't have to work for a team to work in sports.

Most people picture sports business as ticketing, sponsorship, marketing or operations. Those are great careers. But there's another door: the people who measure what sports does for a city and turn it into decisions about money and policy.

Data engineer

Builds the pipelines that pull card, transit, hotel and survey data together, cleans them and keeps them running every year.

SQL · Python · cloud warehouses · APIs

Economic impact analyst

Designs the model, picks defensible inputs and explains the difference between observed, modeled and forecast.

Economics · statistics · Excel · survey design

Data storyteller

Turns a dashboard into a narrative a city council member can act on in five minutes.

Looker · Tableau · Power BI · writing

Policy & funding strategist

Takes the evidence into budget conversations: tourism funds, county grants, state legislation.

Public finance · stakeholder work · presenting

Community impact lead

Makes sure the money reaches small businesses and neighborhoods, then measures whether it did.

Program design · evaluation · partnerships

Sports tech & analytics

Works at teams, leagues, venues or data vendors building the products that create this data in the first place.

Product · analytics engineering · BI

Start this semester

  1. Learn SQL first. It's the language every one of these sources speaks.
  2. Pick one public dataset from your city: transit ridership, hotel occupancy, sales tax. Build one chart that answers one question.
  3. Label your evidence. Practice writing "observed," "modeled" or "forecast" next to every number.
  4. Find the decision. For every analysis, ask: who would spend money differently because of this?
  5. Talk to the people who hold the data. Commissions, chambers, visitor bureaus. They need help more than you think.
